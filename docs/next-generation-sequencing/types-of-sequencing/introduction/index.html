Introduction

The same sequencer can answer very different questions depending on which molecules go into the library. Sequence all of a person's DNA and you can look for variants anywhere. Sequence only the exons and you get the protein-coding parts for a fraction of the cost. Sequence RNA and you learn which genes are switched on. Sequence only the DNA a protein was holding, or only the DNA left open, and you learn how the genome is being used.

Each of these is a separate assay, a laboratory method for measuring one thing, and each has its own way of selecting molecules during library preparation. Choosing one is a tradeoff between how much of the genome you read, how deeply you read it, and how much you can spend. Unless a page says otherwise, the assays here use short reads.

Coverage and Depth​

Two words come up on every assay page. Depth is how many reads cover a given base. Coverage usually means the average depth across the region being sequenced, written with an "x": a genome sequenced to 30x has, on average, 30 reads over each base. Coverage is also used for breadth, the share of the region covered by at least some minimum number of reads, so a report might say "30x mean coverage, with 95% of bases at 20x or more".

Depth matters because no single read can be trusted on its own. With enough reads at a position, a real variant shows up again and again, while a sequencing error shows up in one or two reads and can be set aside.

Since reads fall at random, some bases get more than the average and some get fewer, and a few may get none. If reads landed perfectly at random, the share of bases left with no reads would be about e−c, where c is the coverage (Lander and Waterman, 1988). At 1x, that's about 37% of the genome unread; at 5x, under 1%. Real data is less even than that, because some regions are harder to read than others, so labs sequence deeper than the arithmetic alone suggests.

How deep is deep enough depends on what you're looking for. A germline variant inherited from one parent should be in about half the reads, so a modest depth finds it. A somatic variant in a tumor may be in only a small share of the reads. Normal cells mixed into the sample dilute it, and so do differences among the tumor cells themselves. A tumor usually starts from a single cell, and its descendants keep picking up new variants as they divide (Nowell, 1976). A group of cells descended from one ancestor, and sharing its variants, is a clone. A variant that arose later, in one branch of the tumor, is carried only by that subclone, so it shows up in fewer reads. At 30x, a variant in 10% of the reads is expected in only three of them, too few to tell from an error. Finding variants like that takes much more depth.

The Main Assays​

AssayWhat goes into the libraryWhat it's used for
Whole-genome sequencing (WGS)All of the DNAEvery kind of variant, anywhere in the genome
Whole-exome sequencing (WES)DNA from exons onlyVariants that change proteins, at lower cost
RNA-seqRNA, copied into cDNAWhich genes are on and how much; fusions; splicing
Targeted sequencingDNA from a chosen set of regionsVery deep reads of known genes, often in clinical tests
ChIP-seqDNA held by one proteinWhere a protein binds, or where a histone mark sits
ATAC-seqDNA left open, not wrapped in chromatinWhich regions are open and could be active
Hi-CPieces of DNA that touch in the nucleusHow the genome is folded in three dimensions
Single-cell sequencingMolecules from each cell, kept apart by barcodesWhat differs from one cell to the next

The first four look at the sequence itself: what the DNA says, and what RNA is made from it. ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq and Hi-C look at how the genome is packaged and used, so where the reads land tells you more than their sequence does. Single-cell sequencing is a way of doing other assays, most often RNA-seq, one cell at a time.
