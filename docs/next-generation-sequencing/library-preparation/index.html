Library Preparation

Freshly extracted DNA needs some preparation before it can be sequenced. It is first made into a library: a collection of DNA fragments, each a suitable length and each carrying short synthetic sequences at both ends that the instrument knows how to grab and read from. Building one is called library preparation, and it happens in the wet lab before the sample ever reaches a sequencer.

RNA needs one extra step first. Most sequencers read only DNA, so RNA is copied into complementary DNA (cDNA) by reverse transcriptase, the enzyme retroviruses such as HIV use to copy their RNA genomes into DNA. From there, the cDNA is prepared like any other DNA. Nanopore instruments are the exception, since they can also read RNA directly (Garalde et al., 2018).

The recipe changes with the instrument and with what you want to read, but most libraries go through some version of four steps, roughly in the order below. Those steps grew up around short-read sequencing, and they don't all apply equally to long reads. Short-read and long-read sequencing each get their own page next. For now, the table below shows which steps apply to which, and each section below splits into short reads and long reads.

StepShort readsLong reads
Breaking the DNAInto pieces a few hundred bases longGently, keeping pieces many thousands of bases long
Choosing what to keepCommonPossible, with methods of its own
Adding adapters and indexesAlwaysAlways, with adapters built for the instrument
Making copiesUsually, though often skipped for whole genomesUsually skipped

Breaking the DNA​

The first step is breaking the DNA, and here short and long reads want opposite things.

Short Reads​

Extracted DNA comes in pieces tens of thousands of bases long, while a short-read instrument reads a few hundred bases at most. So the DNA is broken on purpose into fragments of a few hundred bases. There are three popular ways to do it:

  • Shearing breaks DNA with physical force. Long DNA strands are fragile and snap when shaken or squeezed hard enough. The usual tool is sonication, which shakes the DNA with focused sound waves. An older method, nebulization, sprays it through a tiny opening.
  • Enzymatic fragmentation uses a mix of enzymes that cut DNA at scattered spots. It needs no special instrument.
  • Tagmentation uses an enzyme called a transposase, which cuts the DNA and attaches the adapters described below in a single step (Adey et al., 2010). The same enzyme is the heart of ATAC-seq.

The choice often comes down to a lab's equipment and workflow. In one comparison, sonication, nebulization, and enzymes all made good libraries, with only small differences between them (Knierim et al., 2011).

Whichever method is used, each copy of the genome in a sample breaks in different places, so the fragments overlap one another, which is what later lets software piece the reads back together. The breaks are close to random but not perfectly so. Even shearing breaks some sequences more easily than others, which can leave coverage uneven (Poptsova et al., 2014).

Along the way, a size-selection step keeps only fragments in a narrow range, often around 200 to 500 bases.

Long Reads​

For long reads, the goal is to handle the DNA gently and keep the molecules as long as possible. PacBio libraries are still broken, but into pieces many thousands of bases long. For nanopore, read length depends mostly on how intact the extracted DNA is (Logsdon et al., 2020).

Choosing What to Keep​

Many kinds of sequencing also keep only some of the material. This step is called library selection or enrichment. Public archives such as the European Nucleotide Archive ask for it with every sequencing run submitted, using values like "PolyA" or "Hybrid Selection". Unlike the other steps, it has no fixed place in the order.

Short Reads​

The best-known examples are short-read assays, which keep the parts from exons (whole-exome sequencing), RNA with a poly-A tail or all RNA except ribosomal RNA (RNA-seq), or DNA bound to a particular protein (ChIP-seq). RNA-seq and ChIP-seq pull out what they want before library preparation starts, while exome sequencing fishes the exons out of a finished library. This step is what makes an exome an exome, and each page under Common Types of Sequencing explains its own.

Long Reads​

Long reads can be targeted too, mostly with methods of their own. This guide doesn't cover them in depth, but a 2024 review describes each in detail (Iyer et al., 2024). It groups them into four kinds:

  • Long-range PCR uses primers that stick just outside each target, then copies the stretch between them. What's left is a pool of copies of only the targets, each up to about 10 kb, with up to roughly 1,000 targets at once. The copies lose their methylation marks, because PCR builds each new strand from plain, unmarked bases, and the original molecules end up a tiny fraction of the pool.
  • Hybrid capture breaks the DNA into pieces around 10 kb long and mixes them with baits, short strands matching the targets that magnetic beads can grab. The beads pull out the pieces stuck to baits, and everything else is washed away. The captured pieces are then copied by PCR, so methylation marks are lost.
  • Cas9, the DNA-cutting enzyme behind CRISPR, is guided to cut on both sides of each target after the DNA's own ends are blocked. Adapters then favor the targets' fresh ends, so the original target molecules, often tens of kb long, are read with methylation intact. Targets get deep coverage, though stray molecules still make up most reads (Gilpatrick et al., 2020).
  • Adaptive sampling happens during the run, on nanopore instruments only, using an ordinary whole-genome library. Software reads the first few hundred bases of each molecule as it enters the pore, and if it isn't a target, the instrument reverses the voltage to eject it (Payne et al., 2021). Targets are read in full with methylation intact, while everything else leaves only a short stub.

Adding Adapters and Indexes​

Next, short known sequences called adapters are joined to both ends of every fragment. They are what the instrument grabs and reads from, and they can carry an index, a short barcode sequence unique to each sample. Indexes let a lab pool dozens or hundreds of libraries and run them together, called multiplexing. Afterward, software sorts the reads back into samples by their index, called demultiplexing.

Short Reads​

Short-read adapters do three jobs: (1) they let the fragment stick to the sequencer's flow cell, (2) they give the sequencing reaction a known place to start reading, and (3) they carry the index. Figure 4.1 shows a fragment's adapters attaching to a flow cell.

Sorting by index isn't perfect: on some Illumina instruments, a small share of reads pick up another sample's index, a problem called index hopping. One large study measured swap rates of 0.2 to 6% of reads (Costello et al., 2018). Giving each sample a unique pair of indexes, one on each adapter, lets software throw away any read whose pair doesn't match.

Long Reads​

Long-read adapters are built for how each instrument works. PacBio's hairpin adapters close each fragment into a loop (Figure 4.4), and nanopore adapters carry the motor protein that feeds DNA through the pore (Figure 4.5; Logsdon et al., 2020). Both can be multiplexed. PacBio can build indexes into its hairpin adapters (Ardui et al., 2018), and nanopore kits attach an index to both ends of each molecule (Wick et al., 2018).

Making Copies​

The last step is optionally amplifying the library with PCR (polymerase chain reaction), which copies every fragment many times. There are two reasons to do it. First, there may not be enough DNA: libraries made without PCR need a fairly large amount of starting DNA, which many samples can't supply. Second, PCR copies only fragments with adapters on both ends, so it enriches the library for pieces that can actually be sequenced (Aird et al., 2011).

The cost is that copies aren't perfect stand-ins for the original molecules:

  • Duplicates. Copies of the same original fragment get sequenced more than once. They look like independent evidence for whatever they carry, including errors, but they aren't. Software later marks reads that start and end at exactly the same positions as duplicates and counts them once.
  • Bias. Regions very rich or very poor in G and C copy less efficiently, so they end up with fewer reads (Aird et al., 2011).
  • Copying errors. A mistake made in an early PCR cycle is copied into every later copy of that fragment.
  • Lost marks. Copies are built from plain, unmarked bases, so chemical marks on the original DNA, such as methylation, aren't carried over.

Once amplification is done, or skipped, the lab measures the library's concentration and fragment sizes, pools it with others, and loads it onto a sequencer.

Short Reads​

Whether a short-read library is amplified depends on the assay. Capture-based libraries, such as exomes and panels, need PCR after capture (Gnirke et al., 2009), and many tagmentation kits use PCR to finish the adapters and add the indexes (Head et al., 2014). Whole genomes can skip it when there is enough DNA. The 1000 Genomes Project's high-coverage set of 3,202 genomes, for example, was made with PCR-free libraries from 1 µg of DNA each (Byrska-Bishop et al., 2022). When there isn't enough DNA, short random tags called unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) can be attached to each fragment before amplification, so every copy of the same original molecule carries the same tag and can be recognized (Kivioja et al., 2011).

Long Reads​

Long-read libraries usually skip PCR altogether. PCR copies long molecules poorly, rarely beyond about 10 kb (Iyer et al., 2024), and reading the original molecules keeps methylation marks, which both PacBio and nanopore instruments can detect (Logsdon et al., 2020). The main exception is long-range PCR, used to target specific regions as described above.
