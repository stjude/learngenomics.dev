title: What Is Cancer?
---

There were close to 20 million new cases of cancer worldwide in 2022, and 9.7 million
deaths from it ([Bray et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3322/caac.21834)). An estimated
one in five people will develop cancer at some point in their lives. The word covers
well over a hundred different diseases, and in the low to mid hundreds once they are
split by the genetic changes that drive them. They start in different tissues and behave
in very different ways. This page explains what those diseases have in common, how a
cancer begins and how the main types are named. The rest of the chapter looks more
closely at the genes involved and at how a tumor changes as it grows.

## Cells That Stop Cooperating

A human body is built from trillions of cells, and nearly all of them carry the same
genome. Living together takes restraint. A cell divides only when the body needs new
cells, does the specialized job of its tissue and dies when it is damaged or no longer
needed. Neighboring cells, chemical signals and the cell's own genes enforce these
rules.

Cancer is a disease in which some of the body's cells grow uncontrollably and spread to
other parts of the body ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/understanding/what-is-cancer)).
Biologists who study cancer across many species describe it as a breakdown of
cooperation ([Aktipis et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2014.0219)). Cancer
cells divide when they should stop, avoid the death that would remove them, abandon the
specialized work of their tissue, take more than their share of resources and damage the
environment around them. The [next page](02-hallmarks-of-cancer.md) breaks these
abilities down into the hallmarks of cancer.

## Tumors

Extra cells often build up into a lump of tissue called a **tumor**. Not every tumor is
cancer.

A **benign** tumor grows in place. It does not invade nearby tissue or spread, and once
removed it usually does not grow back. Benign tumors can still be dangerous when they
grow large or press on something vital, as some brain tumors do.

A **malignant** tumor is cancer. Its cells **invade** the tissue around them, and some
can break away, travel through the blood or lymph system and start new tumors elsewhere
in the body. This spread is called **metastasis**, and most deaths from cancer are
caused by cancer that has spread. A tumor that grows from cells that have spread keeps
the name of the original, or **primary**, tumor. Breast cancer that spreads to the lung
is metastatic breast cancer, and its cells still look like breast cells under a
microscope.

Some changes come before cancer. In **hyperplasia**, cells divide faster than normal and
build up, but they look normal. In **dysplasia**, the extra cells also look abnormal.
**Carcinoma in situ** is a group of abnormal cells that has not invaded nearby tissue.
It is sometimes called stage 0 cancer, but none of these changes is cancer. Some can
become cancer, so they are often watched or treated ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/understanding/what-is-cancer#tissue-changes-that-are-not-cancer)).

Not every cancer forms a lump. **Leukemias**, cancers of the blood-forming tissue in the
bone marrow, fill the blood and bone marrow with abnormal white blood cells instead.

## How Cancer Starts

Cancer is a genetic disease. It is caused by changes to the genes that control how cells
behave, especially how they grow and divide. Some of these changes are inherited from a
parent. Most arise during a person's lifetime, either from errors made when a cell
copies its DNA before dividing or from DNA damage caused by exposures such as the
chemicals in tobacco smoke and ultraviolet light from the sun. The body normally removes
cells with damaged DNA, but it gets worse at this with age. Age is the single most
important risk factor for cancer, and the median age at diagnosis in the United States
is 67 ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/age)).

A single change is rarely enough. Peter Nowell proposed in 1976 that most tumors arise
from one cell, and that a tumor progresses as its cells acquire new changes and the most
aggressive ones are selected ([Nowell, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.959840)). A
cell gains a change that lets it divide a little more often than its neighbors, so its
descendants slowly outnumber them. One of those descendants gains a second change and
outgrows the rest, and the process repeats. In some tumors, cells eventually gain the
ability to invade. Each new change of this kind sets off another wave of growth, and the
tumor gets larger and more disordered with each one ([Vogelstein and Kinzler,
1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-9525(93)90209-z); [Greaves and Maley,
2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10762)).

The changes that push a cell along this path are called **driver mutations**. Across
more than 7,600 tumors of 29 types, the average tumor carried about four driver
mutations that change a single base in a protein-coding gene. Thyroid and testicular
cancers averaged fewer than one, and colorectal and endometrial cancers more than ten
([Martincorena et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.09.042)). Those counts
leave out larger changes, such as gained or lost chromosome segments, which can drive
cancer too. Most of the other mutations in a tumor are **passengers** that came along
for the ride. [Oncogenes and Tumor Suppressors](03-oncogenes-and-tumor-suppressors.md)
explains which genes the drivers hit, and [How Tumors Evolve](05-how-tumors-evolve.md)
follows the descendants of that first cell further.

Because every tumor follows its own path, each one carries its own combination of
changes, and cells within the same tumor can differ from one another. Two patients with
the same type of cancer can have tumors that are genetically very different.

## Types of Cancer

Cancers are usually named for the organ or tissue where they start, so lung cancer
starts in the lung even if it later spreads to the brain. Worldwide, the most commonly
diagnosed cancers are lung, female breast, colorectal, prostate and stomach cancers
([Bray et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3322/caac.21834)).

Cancers are also grouped by the kind of cell they start in. The main groups are:

- **Carcinomas** start in epithelial cells, which line the inside and outside surfaces
  of the body. They are the most common type of cancer, and most breast, colon, lung and
  prostate cancers are carcinomas.
- **Sarcomas** start in bone and in soft tissues such as muscle, fat and blood vessels.
  Osteosarcoma is the most common cancer of bone.
- **Leukemias** start in the blood-forming tissue of the bone marrow. The four common types
  are acute lymphoblastic, acute myeloid, chronic lymphocytic and chronic myeloid
  leukemia.
- **Lymphomas** start in lymphocytes, the T cells and B cells of the immune system. The
  two main types are Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
- **Multiple myeloma** starts in plasma cells, the immune cells that make antibodies.
- **Melanoma** starts in melanocytes, the cells that make the pigment that colors skin.
  Most melanomas form on the skin.
- **Brain and spinal cord tumors** are named for the cell they start in and where in the
  nervous system they form.

These groups are only the first level. Within each, cancers are split by the exact cell
type and, more and more, by the driver changes they carry. The World Health Organization
now names some brain tumors by their driver, such as IDH-mutant astrocytoma ([Louis et
al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/neuonc/noab106)). Experts don't always agree on where
the lines fall. In 2022, two expert groups published separate classifications of myeloid
leukemias that differ in several places ([Khoury et al.,
2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41375-022-01613-1); [Arber et al.,
2022](https://doi.org/10.1182/blood.2022015850)). That is why one source counts more
than 100 types of cancer ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/understanding/what-is-cancer)) and
another more than 200 ([Cancer Research
UK](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/what-is-cancer)).

## Cancer in Children

Cancer in children is rare. Only about 1% of cancers in the United States are diagnosed
in people under 20 ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/age)). The most
common types are different from those in adults. Leukemias come first, followed by brain
and other central nervous system tumors and then lymphomas ([National Cancer
Institute](https://www.cancer.gov/types/childhood-cancers/child-adolescent-cancers-fact-sheet)).
Carcinomas, so common in adults, are much less common.

The genomes of childhood cancers look different too. A study of 1,699 childhood
leukemias and solid tumors found lower rates of single-base mutations than in common
adult cancers. Of the 142 driver genes it identified, only 45% matched those found in
adult cancers, and most driver events were copy number changes and structural variants
rather than changes to single bases ([Ma et al.,
2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25795)). Some children also carry a germline
variant, present in every cell of the body, that raises their risk. Such variants can be
inherited from a parent or arise new in the child. In a second study of 961 tumors from
children, adolescents and young adults, 7–8% of the patients carried one that clearly
predisposed them to cancer ([Gröbner et al.,
2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25480)). [Inherited Risk](06-inherited-risk.md)
returns to these.
