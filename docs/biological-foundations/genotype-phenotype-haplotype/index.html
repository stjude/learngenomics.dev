Haplotype, Genotype and Phenotype

So far, we've followed a single gene from DNA to a working protein. But as the first page of this chapter explained, chromosomes come in pairs, one inherited from each parent, so nearly every gene comes in two copies. The two copies often differ, and both are transcribed and translated. This page introduces three terms for describing those two copies and what they produce, using the CFTR gene from the previous page as a running example.

Haplotype​

Each copy of a stretch of DNA is a haplotype: the particular sequence on the copy you inherited from one parent. The different versions of the sequence found at one position are called alleles. For CFTR, you might inherit a typical copy from one parent and, from the other, a copy carrying F508del, the three-base deletion that causes the protein to misfold.

Haplotypes matter because variants on the same copy travel together. They were inherited together, and they end up in the same transcript and the same protein.

Genotype​

Genotype is an overloaded term that can mean one of two things:

  • Most commonly within the area of computational genomics, the term genotype refers to the particular alleles found at a given position or locus within the genome. For example, if position 7 on the chromosome in the figure above contains an A on one copy and a G on the other copy, this is said to be a genotype call of A/G at position 7.
  • Less commonly within the area of computational genomics, the term genotype will be used to reference an organism's complete set of genetic material, including all of the variants included therein.

Which definition is implied is largely based on the context of the conversation: if one is talking about a system-wide effect that is likely to be caused by the complicated interaction(s) between various parts of the organism's genetic code, then the second definition is more suited. Within computational genomics, we are often concerned with highly focal changes, hence the preference of the first definition above.

info

Throughout this guide, we'll assume the first definition for genotype unless otherwise specified.

When both copies carry the same allele, the genotype is homozygous; when they differ, it is heterozygous. A person with one typical copy of CFTR and one F508del copy is heterozygous at that position.

Phenotype​

An observable trait is called a phenotype: eye color, height, blood type, or a disease such as cystic fibrosis. Phenotypes come from what proteins do, so the question is what a person's two copies produce together.

For CFTR, one working copy makes enough channel protein to do the job. People with one F508del copy, called carriers, don't have cystic fibrosis; only people with no working copy, such as those with two F508del copies, do. A variant that causes a phenotype only when both copies carry it is called recessive, and cystic fibrosis is a recessive condition. A variant that causes a phenotype with a single copy, often because half the normal amount of protein isn't enough or because the faulty protein interferes with the working one, is called dominant.

Most traits are far less tidy. Height, for example, is shaped by thousands of variants across many genes, plus diet and environment. Untangling these genotype-phenotype relationships is one of the central goals of genomics. In cancer, the features of the disease are the phenotype, and the variants in the patient's genome and tumor make up the genotype, and linking the two helps explain how cancer works.

Same Genotype, Different Haplotypes​

Here's where the three terms come together. Suppose a person is heterozygous for two different harmful variants in CFTR: F508del and G542X, which turns codon 542 into a stop codon. The genotype calls at both positions are the same either way, but the phenotype depends on the haplotypes:

  • If both variants are on the same copy, the other copy is intact. The person is a carrier and, because cystic fibrosis is recessive, doesn't have the disease.
  • If one variant is on each copy, neither copy makes a working protein. The person has cystic fibrosis.

Genotype calls alone can't tell these two cases apart. Working out which variants sit on the same copy is called phasing. Sequencing reads that span both positions can reveal it directly, and so can the genotypes of the person's parents (if one parent carries only F508del and the other carries only G542X, the person must have inherited one variant on each copy).
