Transcription

Genomic Regions and Transcription​

You might expect most of the genome to be genes that encode proteins. In fact, only about 1% of the human genome is coding, meaning it holds the instructions for building proteins. The other 99%, known as "noncoding regions", includes switches that control when genes are used, genes for RNAs that never become proteins, introns (described below), and long stretches of repeated sequence. What much of it does is still an area of active research.

During transcription, an enzyme called RNA polymerase copies the whole gene from DNA to a single strand of immature RNA called pre-mRNA. Before it leaves the nucleus, the pre-mRNA is processed into the finished product, mature mRNA (usually just called mRNA): sections called introns are cut out, and the remaining sections, called exons, are joined together. Most of the exon sequence holds the recipe for the protein, but the stretches at each end, called untranslated regions (UTRs), instead help control how the mRNA is used. A protective cap is added to the front, and a protective tail of many adenines, called the poly-A tail, is added to the back.

Transcription begins at the promoter, a stretch of DNA just before the start of a gene. Proteins called transcription factors recognize short sequences in the promoter, bind to them, and recruit RNA polymerase. Other regions called enhancers, which can sit far from the gene, raise how often it is transcribed: the DNA loops around so that the proteins bound to an enhancer can reach the promoter. Most of these proteins can only reach the DNA where the chromatin around the gene is loose, although a few, called pioneer factors, can bind tightly packed DNA and help open it (see Figure 1.3).

How Transcription Works​

Once RNA polymerase is in place at the promoter, it unwinds a short section of the double helix and reads one of the two strands, called the template strand. It builds the RNA one base at a time, matching each DNA base with its partner: C with G, G with C, T with A, and A with uracil (U), which RNA uses instead of thymine (T). The finished RNA therefore has the same sequence as the other DNA strand, the coding strand, but with U in place of T. The polymerase moves along the gene, letting the DNA close up behind it, until it passes the end of the gene. Even in an active gene, much of the DNA stays wrapped around histones (see Figure 1.6). The polymerase briefly unwraps the DNA from each set of histones as it passes, and the DNA wraps back around them behind it.

info

Because RNA uses U where DNA uses T, every figure and sequence in this guide that shows RNA uses U.

Before it can be used to make proteins, pre-mRNA is processed into mature mRNA in three broad steps. The first two usually begin while RNA polymerase is still copying the gene.

  1. Capping. Soon after the RNA starts to emerge from RNA polymerase, a protective 5′ cap is added to its front end. (To see where the name comes from, read The Structure of DNA.)

  2. Splicing. A large molecular machine called the spliceosome cuts out the introns and joins the exons end to end.

  3. Adding a tail. Once RNA polymerase passes the end of the gene, the RNA is cut free and a long tail of adenines, called the poly-A tail, is added to its back end.

The cap and tail protect the mRNA from being broken down and help ribosomes recognize it. The finished mRNA is then sent out of the nucleus to be translated.

A variant in an exon can change the protein that is made, and so change what it does. Variants outside exons can be just as important: a change in a promoter or enhancer can alter how much a gene is transcribed, and a change near the edge of an exon can alter splicing. The effects of these noncoding variants are harder to interpret, but they can be important in disease. They are covered in Genomic Variation.

Alternative Splicing​

Splicing doesn't always join the same exons. A cell can skip some exons or choose between alternatives, so one gene can produce several different mRNAs, called isoforms, and therefore several versions of its protein. This is called alternative splicing. For the example gene in Figure 1.5, one cell might keep all four exons, while another cell, or the same cell at a different time, keeps only exons 1, 3 and 4, making a protein that may work differently. Nearly all human genes with more than one exon are alternatively spliced. To learn more, read Alternative Splicing.

Bakery Analogy​

Back in the bakery, the staff member (RNA polymerase) copies the whole recipe, including notes in the margins that aren't part of it (introns). Before the index card leaves the back office, the notes are cut out (splicing) and both ends of the card are taped to protect it (the cap and tail). Sometimes a step is left out on purpose to make a variation of the cake (alternative splicing).
